## Construction and Materials

The build is what separates this pendant from the flat, single-tone devotional charms that flood the low end of the market. An oval frame carries the Guadalupe depiction, and the surface is worked in three distinct materials: a central crystal inlay, zircon stones around it, and natural shell accents. Each material reflects light differently — the crystal throws color, the zircon adds sparkle points, and the shell brings a soft organic sheen that keeps the piece from looking synthetic.

That layered approach matters for retail display. Under counter lighting, a multi-texture pendant catches the eye faster than a solid metal charm, and it photographs well for online listings — useful if you're selling through marketplaces where the thumbnail does most of the work.

Setting quality is where lower-cost suppliers usually cut corners, and it's worth understanding how these are assembled. The crystal and zircon components are precision-set to improve light reflection rather than simply glued into a recess. The shell element is what gives the design its artisanal character; no two pieces of natural shell read exactly alike, so expect minor tonal variation between units. For most buyers that's a selling point, not a defect — it supports a "handcrafted" positioning. If your channel requires strict uniformity across a batch, flag that early.

## Pre-Order Verification

Before you commit to a bulk run, nail down the details that aren't visible in a product photo. A few items worth confirming in writing:

- **Physical dimensions and weight.** These aren't listed, so ask for the measured spec sheet — you'll need it for shipping cost calculations and for any retail listing.
- **Chain specification.** Confirm material, length and clasp type, and whether a different length is available at your order volume.
- **Plating and finish durability.** Ask what finish is used on the frame and whether it's suitable for daily-wear customers.
- **Stone security.** Ask how zircon and crystal settings are tested for retention — this is a common failure point on low-cost inlay jewelry.
- **Shell variation tolerance.** Request photos of several units from one batch so you know what natural variation looks like before it arrives.
- **Color consistency.** The palette is central to the design, so ask how color is matched batch to batch.
- **Sample order.** At a 12-piece MOQ, a sample run is the cheapest due diligence available. Order it, handle the piece, then decide.
- **Packaging and labeling.** Confirm what's included by default and what gets added at OEM volume.
- **Lead time and shipping terms.** Both vary by configuration and destination — confirm them for your specific order rather than assuming.

If your organization requires documentation for procurement, ask what the supplier can provide before you place the order, not after.
