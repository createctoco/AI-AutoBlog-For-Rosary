## Material and Design Highlights

Zinc alloy is the right base for this kind of piece, and it's a deliberate choice rather than a cost shortcut. The metal takes a polish well, holds fine detail in the corpus and the cross edges, and gives the ornament enough weight to feel considered in the hand. Buyers who've handled cheap stamped brass or hollow tin items will notice the difference immediately — the zinc alloy body has a solidity that reads as quality at retail.

The enamel work is where most of the visual value sits. Precision inlay lays color into recessed areas of the cross, then the piece is polished so the enamel sits flush with the metal. The result is a glossy, color-rich surface that resists scratching and general wear. Color doesn't sit on top of the metal as a printed layer; it's set into the body. That matters for anything that lives on a dashboard, where sun exposure and dust are constant.

Construction details worth noting: the flat metal base keeps the ornament stable on flat surfaces, so it doesn't slide or tip on a desk or shelf. Weight is balanced — light enough to ship economically in bulk, heavy enough not to feel hollow. And the production process is standardized, which is why consistency holds across bulk runs rather than drifting from batch to batch.

Long-term display is the other consideration. Enamel over zinc alloy doesn't fade the way painted finishes do, and the polished body wipes clean with a dry cloth. For buyers who need an item that still looks presentable after a year on a shelf, that's the relevant spec.

## What to Verify Before Ordering

Start with a physical sample. Photos flatten enamel work, and you can't judge color depth, edge finish, or the weight of the zinc alloy body from a screen. Ask for a production-representative sample rather than a showroom piece, since those two aren't always the same build.

Dimensions and weight per unit aren't fixed across every variant in this line — contact us to confirm the exact figures for the SKU you're planning. You'll need those numbers for carton cube calculations, freight estimates, and any retail listing that requires a size attribute.

Then check the finish consistency question. With enamel inlay, the variables are color accuracy against your approved standard and adhesion at the recessed edges. Ask how the factory handles color approval: is there a signed master sample, and does production get measured against it? For a 1,200-piece custom run, that single step prevents the most common dispute.

Packaging and labeling deserve a written spec. Confirm barcode placement, any required warning text, country-of-origin marking, and whether inner cartons are labeled to your warehouse's receiving standard. Also ask about the inspection process and whether a pre-shipment report with photos is standard or on request.

Finally, get the commercial terms in writing before you commit: price breaks at each quantity tier, sample cost and whether it's credited against the bulk order, production timing for stock versus custom, and the payment structure. None of these should be assumed — all of them vary by order size and configuration.
