## Specs and Build Quality

The construction is zinc alloy with enamel inlay. That combination is the reason these feel heavier in the hand than a stamped brass or thin steel blank would. Zinc alloy takes detail well, so the surface relief on the religious motifs stays crisp, and the enamel fills add color contrast that survives repeated handling better than paint would.

A few build points worth understanding before you buy:

**Weight and feel.** A keychain that feels substantial reads as quality to the end customer. The weighty profile here is intentional — it's the difference between something that feels like a trinket and something that feels like a keepsake.

**Antique plating.** The retro finish isn't a surface defect; it's a deliberate treatment. Buyers sourcing vintage-inspired religious goods tend to want that aged look, so make sure your sample review accounts for it rather than flagging it as inconsistency.

**Dual hardware.** Since these double as pendants, the attachment point has to handle both a key ring and a cord or chain. That's a functional detail to check on samples — the loop needs to be clean and closed.

**Handling durability.** The pieces are described as reliable for repeated handling, which is what you'd expect from a metal body with enamel fill. Whether that meets your specific returns threshold depends on your market, so test it yourself.

## Checklist Before You Commit

Sourcing metal-and-enamel goods has a few predictable friction points. Work through these before you sign off on a bulk order.

**Verify the enamel finish on a physical sample.** Photos flatter enamel. Check for bubbles, pooling at the edges of the motif, and color consistency across units in the same design. If you're ordering multiple motifs, check that the palette reads as a coherent family.

**Confirm the attachment hardware.** Because these are dual-purpose, inspect the loop or jump ring. Ask whether the pendant hardware is rated for a cord and whether the key ring is included or sold separately.

**Check plating tone against your existing line.** "Antique" covers a range. If your store already stocks brass-toned and silver-toned religious goods, confirm which tone each style uses so you don't end up with a mixed display that looks accidental.

**Ask about packaging options and their effect on MOQ.** Custom packaging may change the minimum. Get that in writing before you plan a launch date.

**Request the spec sheet for the styles you've shortlisted.** Dimensions, weight per piece, and carton configuration all affect your freight math. We'll confirm these per item — they vary across the range.

**Test the dual-use claim with real end users if you can.** Hand a few samples to staff or volunteers and see whether they wear them or keyring them. That tells you how to merchandise the line.

One more: ask about the returns profile on comparable items. Durable construction reduces wear-related returns, but your own market's expectations are the real benchmark.
