## Material and Design Highlights

The 18K gold plating is the commercial center of gravity here. It gives the piece a warm, deep luster that reads closer to heirloom than to bright costume gold, which fits the vintage brief. Plating also carries a durability claim worth understanding: the finish resists tarnishing, so the pendant holds its appearance through storage, display handling, and everyday wear. For a retailer whose stock sits in a case or a warehouse for months before it sells, that matters more than it does for a fast-turnover fashion item.

Design-wise, the vintage detailing on the Virgin Mary image is what separates this pendant from generic religious stock. The figure is devotional rather than decorative — the kind of rendering a parishioner recognizes immediately and a buyer can justify pricing around.

Then there's the compatibility question. The pendant is designed to pair with a variety of chains, which is a bigger deal for wholesale buyers than it sounds. You're not locked into one supplied chain; you can pair it with a fine cable chain for the gift market, something heavier for a men's line, or skip the chain entirely and sell the pendant as a component for DIY rosary and necklace assembly. That flexibility lets a single SKU serve several price points in your catalog.

Durability is built for everyday wear, which is the right target for devotional jewelry. These pieces get worn daily, not occasionally, and a finish that fails in three weeks generates returns nobody wants to process.

## What to Verify Before Ordering

A pendant photograph and a pendant in your hand are two different products. Before you commit to bulk, run through the details that don't show up in a web listing.

Start with physical specs. Chain length, pendant dimensions, weight, clasp type, and the base metal under the plating all need confirming — contact us for exact specifications on any of these, since they vary by configuration. If you're selling into markets with nickel-sensitivity regulations, ask directly about the composition of the findings and the plating chemistry before you order.

Sample first. A pre-production sample tells you more about the plating color, the depth of the vintage detailing, and the finish quality than any spec sheet will. Compare it against your existing inventory under your own store lighting.

Check consistency terms. Ask how color and finish are controlled across production batches — if you're reordering in six months, you want the second run to match the first.

Packaging and labeling are easy to overlook and expensive to fix later. Confirm what's included as standard, what's an upgrade, and whether barcodes, hang tags, or church-specific inserts can be added.

Finally, nail down MOQ, bulk pricing tiers, and production timelines in writing. Lead times vary by order size and customization level, so get the schedule confirmed for your specific quantity rather than accepting a general figure.
