## Specs and Build Quality

Metal construction is the headline. The source spec is short and specific — durable metal, detailed cross design, polished to a smooth and lasting sheen. Those three attributes matter for wholesale because they map onto handling, recognition and shelf life.

Where an item gets picked up, set down, dusted and picked up again, metal earns its keep. Wood chips at the edges; resin can scuff chalky. Metal holds a polish and tolerates repeated contact. For a parish handing the same design to two hundred congregants, that's the difference between a gift people keep and one that ends up in a drawer by spring.

The finish also does quiet work on a display shelf. Under retail lighting it catches a little reflection, which is often all a small desktop piece needs to draw a second look without taking up much footprint.

Exact dimensions, weight and finish variants aren't fixed across the line — these vary by item and by the finish you select. Contact us with your target size range and we'll confirm what's actually available, rather than quoting numbers that may not apply to your configuration.

If your order splits across production runs, ask how plating and polishing are controlled between batches. It's a common complaint among buyers reselling religious metalwork, and it's far easier to settle at sample stage than after the container lands.

## Checklist Before You Commit

Samples first. Always. Order one in each finish you're weighing and handle it the way a customer would — pick it up, set it down, look at it under the lighting your shelf actually uses.

Then work through these before the purchase order goes out:

- **Finish consistency** — how is plating and polishing controlled between batches?
- **Base stability** — does it sit flat, and does it tip if nudged?
- **Packaging spec** — what's the standard inner and outer pack, and can it be changed?
- **Logo method** — engraving, printing or a tag, and which finishes accept it cleanly?
- **Labeling** — can retail-ready labels or barcodes be applied at factory level?
- **Batch matching** — if you split an order across months, will the finish still match?
- **Sample-to-bulk parity** — is the bulk unit identical to the approved sample?

Wondering whether a small trial order makes sense before a 1200-piece commitment? Usually it does. A 12-piece run in stock finishes lets you test sell-through in a real shop, or put a few pieces in front of a church contact, before capital gets tied up.

One caveat on timing: don't assume any lead time applies to your order until it's confirmed in writing. Production windows shift with finish complexity and season, and verbal ranges have a way of becoming expectations.
